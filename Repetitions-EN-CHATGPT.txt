The protests started in Minneapolis, Minnesota, and later spread to cities including Los Angeles, New York City, San Francisco, Boston, Durham, Denver, Tucson, and Washington, D.C.   
The immediate trigger for the protests was the fatal shooting of two U.S. citizens during federal immigration operations in Minneapolis. Renée Good was killed on January 7, 2026, during an ICE operation, and Alex Pretti, an intensive care nurse, was killed on January 24 during a separate federal enforcement action. Video footage and witness accounts circulated widely online, leading to national attention and public criticism of federal immigration tactics.   
Demonstrators included immigrant-rights groups, student organizations, labor unions, faith leaders, civil-rights advocates, and local community members. Organizations involved reportedly included labor unions such as AFSCME Local 3800 and student groups connected to the University of Minnesota. Some national activist organizations also participated in rallies and marches.  
The protests took several forms, including marches, rallies, vigils, school walkouts, business closures, and calls for a nationwide general strike under slogans such as “ICE Out” and “No work, no school, no shopping.” While many demonstrations were peaceful, some cities reported clashes between protesters and police, vandalism, road blockages, and arrests.   
Protesters demanded investigations into the shootings, limits on ICE operations, stronger protections for immigrants and students, and, in some cases, the abolition or defunding of ICE. Federal officials defended immigration enforcement operations and maintained that agents acted lawfully. Public opinion remained divided, though some polling suggested that many Americans viewed the demonstrations as legitimate protests rather than unlawful activity.

According to reports by Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, Iranian authorities deployed the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), Basij militias, police forces known as FARAJA, and plainclothes agents to suppress protesters across the country. Security forces used live ammunition, rifles, shotguns loaded with metal pellets, tear gas, water cannons, and severe beatings against largely peaceful demonstrators. Witness accounts and verified videos indicated that snipers and armed personnel fired from rooftops, mosques, police stations, and military positions directly into crowds, often targeting protesters’ heads, faces, and torsos.
Human rights groups documented mass killings and injuries, including the deaths of children and bystanders. Amnesty International described the events of January 8–9, 2026, as among the deadliest episodes of repression in Iran in decades. Thousands of protesters were reportedly killed or wounded, while tens of thousands were arbitrarily arrested. Detainees faced torture, enforced disappearances, denial of medical treatment, and abusive interrogations.
The Iranian government also imposed a near-total internet blackout beginning on January 8, 2026, severely restricting communications and preventing the documentation of abuses. Rights organizations argued that the blackout was intended to conceal state violence from the international community.
International observers and human rights advocates accused Iranian authorities of committing serious violations of international law, with some organizations describing the repression as potentially amounting to crimes against humanity.
